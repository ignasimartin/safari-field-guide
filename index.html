African Wildcat

Felis lybica

Nocturnal

The wild ancestor of the domestic cat, closely resembling a lean tabby but with longer legs, reddish backs to the ears, and a far warier manner. Easily overlooked in the grass at dusk.

See photos
Field notes
  • All domestic cats are believed to descend from this species, first tamed in North Africa around 10,000 years ago.
  • Hunts alone at night for rodents, birds and insects, relying on stealth rather than speed.
  • Hybridises readily with domestic cats near villages, a real conservation concern for the pure wild form.

Caracal

Caracal caracal

Nocturnal

Medium-sized cat with distinctive long black ear tufts and a robust, reddish-tan coat. Incredibly agile, capable of leaping 3 metres into the air to catch birds in flight.

See photos
Field notes
  • Powerful hind legs allow vertical leaps to snatch birds mid-flight
  • Mostly nocturnal and solitary, rarely seen on safari
  • Name derives from Turkish "karakulak" meaning black ear
  • Can take prey up to three times its own weight

Cheetah

Acinonyx jubatus

Predator

The fastest land animal, built for the sprint with a deep chest, slim waist and a long rudder-like tail. It hunts by day to avoid competition from stronger nocturnal predators. Botswana's open plains are a global stronghold.

See photos
Field notes
  • Accelerates from 0 to 100 km/h in about three seconds.
  • Semi-retractable claws act like running spikes — the reason its tracks show claw marks.
  • The dark 'tear marks' are thought to cut sun glare while hunting.

Leopard

Panthera pardus

Big Five

The most elusive of the big cats, solitary and superbly camouflaged. Powerful and stealthy, it hauls kills high into trees to keep them from lions and hyenas. Riverine woodland along the Okavango and Chobe offers the best sightings.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can carry prey heavier than itself vertically up a tree trunk.
  • Each rosette pattern is unique, like a fingerprint.
  • Thrives closer to human settlement than any other big cat, thanks to its secrecy.

Lion

Panthera leo

Big Five

The apex social cat of the savanna, living in prides of related females with their cubs and a coalition of males. Largely nocturnal hunters, they spend up to 20 hours a day resting in shade.

See photos
Field notes
  • A male's roar carries over 8 km and is used to advertise territory.
  • Females do most of the cooperative hunting; males defend the pride and its range.
  • The Kalahari's black-maned lions are famous for surviving in near-waterless desert.

Serval

Leptailurus serval

Nocturnal

A slender, long-legged cat of tall grassland and wetland edges, golden-yellow with bold black spots and very large oval ears. Servals hunt by leaping high into the air and pouncing down on rodents hidden in grass.

See photos
Field notes
  • Catches prey with a success rate of around 50%, among the highest of any cat.
  • Can leap over 2 metres straight up to snatch birds in flight.
  • Mostly nocturnal but often active at dawn and dusk near water.

African Wild Dog

Lycaon pictus

Endangered

Africa's most efficient predator and one of its most endangered, hunting in tightly cooperative packs with a success rate above 80%. Each dog's blotched coat of black, tan and white is unique. Botswana and Zimbabwe are among its last true strongholds.

See photos
Field notes
  • Also called the painted wolf; no two individuals share the same coat pattern.
  • Packs 'vote' on whether to hunt by sneezing.
  • Rounded, oversized ears radiate heat and pick up pack calls over long distances.

Black-backed Jackal

Lupulella mesomelas

Nocturnal

A slender, sharp-faced canid named for the dark saddle of black-and-silver hair down its back. Adaptable and clever, it hunts small prey, scavenges at kills and forms lifelong pairs that defend a territory together.

See photos
Field notes
  • Pairs mate for life and hold territory year-round.
  • Its high, wavering call is one of the classic sounds of the African night.
  • Bold enough to dart in and snatch scraps from beneath feeding lions.

Side-striped Jackal

Lupulella adusta

Nocturnal

More retiring than its black-backed cousin, this jackal has a pale side-stripe and white-tipped tail. Prefers well-watered woodland and is often heard calling at dusk.

See photos
Field notes
  • More omnivorous than other jackals, eating fruit, insects, and small vertebrates
  • White-tipped tail distinguishes it from the black-backed jackal
  • Nocturnal and rarely seen during daytime game drives
  • Often found near water sources in moist savanna

Spotted Hyena

Crocuta crocuta

Ugly Five

Far more than a scavenger — a formidable hunter that also runs cackling clans of up to 80 animals led by dominant females. The sloping back comes from powerful, high shoulders. Its jaws are among the strongest of any mammal.

See photos
Field notes
  • Bone-crushing bite force lets it digest hooves, horn and bone.
  • Clans are matriarchal; females outrank all males.
  • The eerie 'whooping' call at night is a long-distance contact between clan members.

Aardwolf

Proteles cristata

Nocturnal

A shy, insect-eating member of the hyena family, patterned like a small striped hyena but built for lapping up termites rather than scavenging carcasses. Strictly nocturnal and easily missed even where it is common.

See photos
Field notes
  • Feeds almost exclusively on harvester termites, lapping up tens of thousands with a long sticky tongue in a single night.
  • Despite its hyena-like looks, its teeth are small and peg-like, unable to crush bone.
  • Marks territory with scent rather than calls, and often shelters in old aardvark burrows.

African Civet

Civettictis civetta

Nocturnal

Large, stocky nocturnal carnivore with bold black-and-white blotches and a striking black face mask. Produces civetone, historically used in perfumery.

See photos
Field notes
  • Strictly nocturnal, best spotted on night drives
  • Deposits musk from perineal glands at communal "civet stations"
  • Omnivorous diet includes fruit, insects, rodents, and carrion
  • Not a true cat despite its name — closer to mongooses

Banded Mongoose

Mungos mungo

Predator

A small, sociable mongoose that lives in busy foraging bands of a dozen or more, sweeping through the bush turning over dung and litter for insects. A row of dark bands across the back gives it its name.

See photos
Field notes
  • Whole packs mob snakes and raptors, driving off threats far bigger than themselves.
  • Groups keep up a constant low chatter to stay coordinated while foraging.
  • They den communally in termite mounds and old burrows.

Bat-eared Fox

Otocyon megalotis

Nocturnal

A dainty, sandy fox with enormous ears out of all proportion to its head. Largely insect-eating, it hunts by night in the arid Kalahari, cocking those huge ears to the ground to pinpoint termites and beetle larvae.

See photos
Field notes
  • Ears up to 13 cm long detect insects moving underground.
  • Has more teeth than almost any other placental mammal — around 48.
  • Pairs are devoted and often forage side by side for life.

Cape Clawless Otter

Aonyx capensis

Predator

Africa's largest freshwater otter, with distinctive white facial markings and partially webbed, clawless forepaws used to probe under rocks for crabs and fish.

See photos
Field notes
  • Fingers lack claws, giving exceptional dexterity for handling prey
  • Feeds mainly on crabs, frogs, and fish
  • Found along rivers, dams, and estuaries across southern Africa
  • Can hold its breath for up to two minutes while diving

Common Dwarf Mongoose

Helogale parvula

Predator

Africa's smallest carnivore, a glossy chestnut-brown mongoose that lives in busy family groups of up to 30 in old termite mounds. Highly social, with a dominant breeding pair and cooperative sentries.

See photos
Field notes
  • Maintains a mutualistic relationship with hornbills — the birds act as extra lookouts while both forage.
  • Troops rotate through several termite-mound dens within their territory.
  • Grooms and plays extensively to reinforce group bonds.

Genet

Genetta genetta

Nocturnal

A slender, cat-like nocturnal hunter with a spotted coat and a strikingly long, ringed tail — often caught frozen in a spotlight beam on a night drive, or raiding a camp's rafters for scraps after dark.

See photos
Field notes
  • Almost entirely nocturnal and largely solitary, spending the day curled up in a tree hollow or dense thicket.
  • An agile climber that hunts insects, rodents and small birds, using its long tail for balance along branches.
  • Often becomes surprisingly tame around safari camps, visiting dining areas after dark for easy scraps.

Honey Badger

Mellivora capensis

Nocturnal

A small, low-slung carnivore of legendary toughness, with a grey mantle over a black body. Fearless and immensely strong for its size, it will dig, climb and raid nests, and stand its ground against animals many times larger.

See photos
Field notes
  • Famous for attacking bee hives for honey and larvae, shrugging off stings.
  • Loose, rubbery skin lets it twist around and bite a predator that has it by the neck.
  • Often followed by a honeyguide bird that leads it to hives.

Meerkat

Suricata suricatta

Predator

An iconic Kalahari specialist that lives in cooperative 'mobs', taking turns to stand sentry on hind legs while the rest dig for insects and scorpions. A famously tight-knit social system built on shared duties.

See photos
Field notes
  • Sentinels bark a running commentary of alarm calls while the group forages.
  • Dark eye patches cut glare, like a footballer's eye black, letting them scan for eagles.
  • Immune to the venom of the scorpions they routinely eat.

Slender Mongoose

Herpestes sanguineus

Predator

A sleek, solitary mongoose with a distinctive black-tipped tail, usually seen dashing low across roads or through undergrowth. Varies from grey-brown to dark reddish-brown depending on habitat.

See photos
Field notes
  • Almost always solitary, unlike most other mongoose species in the region.
  • Moves with a characteristic low, sinuous gait, tail curved upward.
  • Hunts insects, lizards, small rodents, and occasionally snakes.

Water Mongoose

Atilax paludinosus

Nocturnal

A large, shaggy, dark-brown mongoose that lives along rivers, marshes, and dams. Mostly nocturnal and solitary, it forages by feeling under rocks and in mud with its dexterous, unwebbed fingers.

See photos
Field notes
  • Cracks open crabs and mussels by hurling them against rocks — one of the few tool-using carnivores.
  • Excellent swimmer, hunting frogs, fish, and aquatic invertebrates.
  • Leaves distinctive star-shaped tracks along muddy banks.

Yellow Mongoose

Cynictis penicillata

Predator

A small, sandy-yellow mongoose of drier grasslands and scrub, often seen sitting upright at its burrow entrance. Lives in loose colonies that frequently share warrens with meerkats and ground squirrels.

See photos
Field notes
  • Sits bolt upright on its haunches to scan for predators — often the first animal spotted at a shared burrow.
  • Digs its own burrows but readily moves into disused springhare or ground-squirrel holes.
  • Primarily insectivorous but also takes small vertebrates and eggs.

Baboon

Papio spp.

Omnivore

A large, intelligent and highly social ground primate living in troops with a strict hierarchy. Bold and opportunistic, chacmas raid, forage and range widely, and act as sentinels whose alarm barks alert the whole bush.

See photos
Field notes
  • The largest baboon species and one of the heaviest of all monkeys.
  • Troops post lookouts and give distinct alarm calls for different predators.
  • Notorious camp raiders — they can open zips, latches and cooler boxes.
  • Three regional species: chacma in the south, olive across East and Central Africa, and yellow along the south-east.

Southern Lesser Galago

Galago moholi

Nocturnal

A tiny, nocturnal primate with enormous round eyes and bat-like ears, common in woodland but rarely seen — more often heard giving its loud, wailing cry at night. Also known as the bushbaby.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can leap up to 5 metres between branches thanks to powerful elongated ankle bones.
  • Marks branches with urine on its hands and feet for scent-trail navigation.
  • Catches flying insects with rapid two-handed grabs.

Vervet Monkey

Chlorocebus pygerythrus

Omnivore

A small, quick grey monkey with a black face, common wherever there are trees and water. Troops are lively and inquisitive, foraging on fruit, seeds and insects, and never far from a raid on an unattended breakfast.

See photos
Field notes
  • Uses distinct alarm calls for leopard, eagle and snake — an early study of animal 'words'.
  • Males are recognised by their vivid blue scrotum, a signal of status.
  • Highly adaptable, thriving alongside people around lodges and villages.

African Bush Elephant

Loxodonta africana

Big Five

The largest land animal on Earth, unmistakable in Chobe and the Okavango, which together hold some of Africa's densest herds. Cows and calves move in tight matriarchal families; older bulls roam alone or in loose bachelor groups.

See photos
Field notes
  • A single adult eats up to 150 kg of vegetation and drinks ~200 litres of water a day.
  • Herds communicate over kilometres using deep infrasonic rumbles below human hearing.
  • Northern Botswana holds the largest elephant population of any country on the continent.

Black Rhinoceros

Diceros bicornis

Critically endangered

Smaller, rarer and more aggressive than the white rhino, with a pointed, prehensile upper lip for browsing leaves and twigs. It holds its head higher and is usually solitary and secretive in thick bush.

See photos
Field notes
  • The hooked lip is a browser's tool — it strips branches rather than grazing grass.
  • Poor eyesight but an excellent sense of smell and hearing.
  • One of the world's most endangered large mammals, with fewer than 6,500 left.

Cape Buffalo

Syncerus caffer

Big Five

A massive, unpredictable grazer that lives in herds hundreds strong, while lone old bulls — the notorious 'dagga boys' — turn dangerously bad-tempered. Its fused horn base, or boss, forms a natural helmet.

See photos
Field notes
  • Widely regarded as one of Africa's most dangerous animals to humans.
  • Herds mob predators and will chase lions off a kill.
  • Old solitary bulls wallow in mud, earning the name 'dagga (mud) boys'.

Hippopotamus

Hippopotamus amphibius

Trauma

Semi-aquatic and deceptively dangerous, resting by day in pods in rivers and pans and grazing on land at night. Highly territorial in water, it is responsible for more human deaths in Africa than any other large animal.

See photos
Field notes
  • Cannot actually swim — it walks and pushes off the bottom.
  • Secretes a red oily 'blood sweat' that works as sunscreen and antiseptic.
  • Can hold its breath underwater for around five minutes.

Plains Zebra

Equus quagga

Grazer

The classic striped grazer of the savanna, moving in family harems of a stallion, mares and foals that often mass into large mixed herds. In parts of Botswana, zebra undertake one of Africa's longest overland migrations.

See photos
Field notes
  • Every zebra's stripe pattern is unique — foals learn to recognise their mother's.
  • Stripes are thought to deter biting flies and confuse predators in a running herd.
  • Botswana's zebra migrate hundreds of kilometres between the Okavango and Makgadikgadi.

Southern Giraffe

Giraffa giraffa

Browser

The tallest animal alive, browsing acacia canopy that nothing else can reach thanks to a prehensile tongue up to 45 cm long. It moves in loose, ever-shifting herds across the woodlands of both countries.

See photos
Field notes
  • Has the same seven neck vertebrae as humans — just hugely elongated.
  • A special valve system stops it blacking out when it raises its head from drinking.
  • Necks are used as weapons in male 'necking' contests for dominance.
  • In Zambia's South Luangwa lives the isolated Thornicroft's giraffe, a local form of this southern group.

Warthog

Phacochoerus africanus

Ugly Five

A comical, warty pig of the open grassland that grazes on bent 'wrists', trotting off with its thin tail held bolt upright like an aerial. It shelters and breeds in abandoned aardvark burrows, reversing in tail-first.

See photos
Field notes
  • Runs with its tail held straight up so piglets can follow through tall grass.
  • Backs into burrows so its tusks face any intruder.
  • The facial 'warts' are protective pads that cushion blows during fights.

White Rhinoceros

Ceratotherium simum

Big Five

The larger of Africa's two rhinos, a grassland grazer with a broad square lip built for cropping short grass. Placid and near-sighted, it lives singly or in small groups. Both rhino species are under intense pressure from poaching.

See photos
Field notes
  • 'White' is a mistranslation of the Afrikaans/Dutch 'wijd' (wide), for its lip.
  • The front horn can exceed 1.5 m and is made of keratin, like hair and nails.
  • Reintroduced populations are carefully protected across Botswana and Zimbabwe.

Blue Wildebeest

Connochaetes taurinus

Ugly Five

A gregarious grazer often mistaken for an antelope but built like an ox, with a heavy head, mane and beard. Herds gather in their thousands and mingle with zebra, sharing sharp senses that warn against predators.

See photos
Field notes
  • Also called the gnu, after the sound of its snorting call.
  • Highly synchronised breeding floods the plains with calves within a few weeks.
  • Calves can stand and run within minutes of birth.

Bontebok / Blesbok

Damaliscus pygargus

Grazer

A striking chocolate-brown antelope with a glossy white blaze down the face and white underparts. It is a South African Cape endemic rather than a natural resident of Botswana or Zimbabwe, so it is only found here on reserves that have introduced it outside its historic range.

See photos
Field notes
  • Nearly hunted to extinction in the 19th century; every bontebok alive today descends from a small herd protected on a single Cape farm.
  • The blesbok, a paler highveld subspecies, is more widely translocated onto game farms across the region than the true Cape bontebok.
  • Both sexes carry ringed, lyre-shaped horns and mark territories with communal dung middens.

Bush Duiker

Sylvicapra grimmia

Browser

The most widespread of Africa's duikers — a small grey-brown antelope of bush, woodland edge and even farmland, named for its habit of ducking ("duiker" is Afrikaans for diver) into cover when startled.

See photos
Field notes
  • Unlike most duikers, it favours drier, more open habitat rather than dense forest.
  • An opportunistic omnivore that will occasionally eat insects, carrion and even young birds.
  • Males carry short, straight horns; females are usually hornless.

Bushbuck

Tragelaphus scriptus

Browser

A shy, solitary antelope of thick riverine bush and forest edges, more often heard crashing off through the undergrowth than seen. Males carry short spiral horns; both sexes wear a chestnut coat with a white throat patch, spots and stripes as individual as a fingerprint.

See photos
Field notes
  • Relies on camouflage and stillness rather than speed, freezing flat against cover when alarmed.
  • White spots, stripes and throat patch form a marking pattern unique to each individual.
  • Mostly active at dawn, dusk and night; rests hidden in dense thickets through the heat of the day.

Common Eland

Taurotragus oryx

Browser

Africa's largest antelope — a massive, ox-like animal with spiralled horns and a heavy dewlap. Despite their bulk, elands are powerful jumpers, able to clear over two metres from a standstill.

See photos
Field notes
  • A loud clicking from the knees as bulls walk is thought to advertise their size to rivals.
  • Highly nomadic, roaming widely in loose herds that can number in the hundreds.
  • Efficient water users, able to go long periods on moisture obtained from browse alone.

Common Reedbuck

Redunca arundinum

Grazer

A medium-sized, sandy-brown antelope found in pairs or small groups in tall grassland and reedbeds near water. Males carry short, forward-curved horns. Gives a distinctive sharp whistle when alarmed.

See photos
Field notes
  • Drops flat into tall grass when threatened, flushing only at the last moment.
  • Strongly territorial males mark their range with a dark, waxy secretion from a gland below the ears.
  • Usually grazes at night and rests in thick cover during the day.
  • The northern bohor reedbuck replaces it from East Africa northward, a close counterpart with shorter horns.

Gemsbok / Beisa Oryx

Oryx gazella / O. beisa

Grazer

A striking desert-adapted antelope of the Kalahari sandveld, marked with bold black-and-white facial patterns and long, rapier-straight horns carried by both sexes. Gemsbok can go extended periods without drinking.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can tolerate a body temperature several degrees above the normal mammalian limit, reducing water loss through sweating.
  • Their straight horns, up to 1.2 m long, are formidable weapons even against lions.
  • Often seen alone or in small groups far from any permanent water in the Kalahari's deep sand country.
  • The gemsbok of the Kalahari and Namib is replaced in the Horn and East Africa by the very similar beisa oryx.

Grant's Gazelle

Nanger granti

Grazer

Tall, elegant gazelle of the East African plains with long lyre-shaped horns and a white rump patch that extends above the tail. Highly drought-tolerant.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can survive without drinking water, getting moisture entirely from food
  • Distinguished from Thomson's gazelle by larger size and white rump extending above the tail
  • Males defend territories with elaborate horn displays
  • Common on open grasslands and semi-arid plains of East Africa

Greater Kudu

Tragelaphus strepsiceros

Browser

A tall, elegant woodland browser with a grey-brown coat marked by thin white stripes. Bulls carry magnificent spiralled horns; the shy, hornless females melt into thick bush. It bounds over obstacles with astonishing agility.

See photos
Field notes
  • A big bull's spiral horns can reach 1.8 m along the curve.
  • Nicknamed the 'grey ghost' for its ability to vanish into bushveld.
  • Can clear fences over 2.5 m high from a standing start.

Hartebeest

Alcelaphus buselaphus

Grazer

A tall, angular antelope with an elongated face and high-mounted, ring-horns, built for stamina rather than speed. Lichtenstein's hartebeest, shown here, is the subspecies typical of Zimbabwe's woodlands and northern Botswana; the paler red hartebeest takes over further into the Kalahari.

See photos
Field notes
  • One of the most tireless runners of the savanna, able to sustain a fast pace far longer than most predators.
  • Herds often post a sentry on a termite mound to watch for approaching danger.
  • Its oddly shaped skull and shoulder hump give an ungainly silhouette that is still unmistakable at a distance.
  • Several regional forms: red hartebeest in the arid south-west, Coke's (kongoni) across East Africa, and Lichtenstein's in the miombo woodlands.

Impala

Aepyceros melampus

Grazer

The graceful, ubiquitous antelope of the bushveld and a staple prey for nearly every predator. Rams hold breeding harems in the rains and gather in bachelor herds otherwise. When alarmed, a herd explodes in soaring leaps.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can leap up to 3 m high and 10 m in a single bound to confuse predators.
  • Distinctive black 'M' marking on the rump gives it the nickname 'McDonald's of the bush'.
  • Scent glands on the hind legs release a trail that keeps scattered herds together.

Kirk's Dik-dik

Madoqua kirkii

Browser

Tiny antelope standing just 35 cm tall, with enormous dark eyes, a flexible proboscis-like snout, and a tuft of reddish hair between small horns (males only).

See photos
Field notes
  • Named for its alarm call — a sharp "dik-dik" whistle
  • Mates for life; pairs defend tiny territories of about 5 hectares
  • Prerineal glands below the eyes produce a sticky black secretion used to mark territory
  • One of Africa's smallest antelopes, weighing only 3–6 kg

Klipspringer

Oreotragus oreotragus

Browser

A compact, sure-footed antelope built for rocky outcrops and koppies, standing on the tips of unusually rounded, rubbery hooves. Pairs mate for life and are almost always seen together, alert on some prominent rock.

See photos
Field notes
  • Walks on the very tips of its hooves — a grip adaptation unique among antelope for bare rock.
  • Its dense, speckled coat insulates against the sharp temperature swings of exposed rock habitats.
  • A pair keeps constant watch in turns, one animal always scanning while the other feeds.

Nyala

Tragelaphus angasii

Browser

A shy, elegant spiral-horned antelope of dense thicket and riverine woodland in the region's eastern lowveld. Males are slate-grey with a shaggy fringe and faint white stripes; females and young are chestnut and hornless, looking almost like a different species.

See photos
Field notes
  • Males raise a crest of long hair along the spine and throat when alarmed or displaying to rivals.
  • Strong sexual dimorphism: mature bulls can weigh nearly twice as much as females.
  • Prefers thick cover near water and is most active at dawn, dusk and after dark.

Puku

Kobus vardonii

Grazer

A stocky, golden-yellow antelope of floodplain fringes, similar to the impala but heavier-built, without the impala's black side-stripe. Males carry short, lyre-shaped horns. Restricted mainly to the Chobe floodplain in Botswana.

See photos
Field notes
  • One of the rarest antelope in southern Africa, with its core population along the Chobe River.
  • Rarely strays far from water, retreating into shallow flood zones when threatened.
  • Males defend small territorial patches on the floodplain and whistle loudly to advertise.

Red Lechwe

Kobus leche

Near threatened

A rich chestnut antelope built for wetlands, with long legs and splayed hooves for grip on soft, waterlogged ground. Utterly at home on the Okavango's floodplains, often grazing belly-deep or bounding through shallow water in long, splashing leaps.

See photos
Field notes
  • Gathers in large mixed herds on the seasonal floodplains of the Okavango and Chobe–Linyanti systems.
  • Powerful hindquarters let it outrun predators through water with spectacular high, splashing leaps.
  • Rams display and rut on traditional gathering grounds known as leks during the breeding season.
  • Zambia holds two local races: the Kafue lechwe of the Kafue Flats and the black lechwe of Lake Bangweulu.

Roan Antelope

Hippotragus equinus

Grazer

One of Africa's largest antelope, horse-faced and grey-brown with a pale belly, bold black-and-white facial markings and short backswept horns on both sexes. Lives in small herds on open woodland and grassland, now scarce outside well-protected core areas.

See photos
Field notes
  • Second in size only to the eland among Africa's antelope.
  • Rival bulls fight on their knees, clashing ridged, backward-curving horns.
  • Highly sensitive to grazing competition and disturbance; strongholds are now largely confined to reserves.

Sable Antelope

Hippotragus niger

Grazer

One of Africa's most striking antelope: glossy black bulls with a white belly and face mask, and huge scimitar horns that sweep back in a bold arc. It favours open woodland and is a prized sighting in Zimbabwe's Hwange.

See photos
Field notes
  • Both sexes bear horns, but a dominant bull's can exceed 1.5 m.
  • When cornered, a sable will drop to its knees and slash at predators with its horns.
  • Bulls darken from chestnut to jet black as they mature.

Sitatunga

Tragelaphus spekii

Browser

A secretive, semi-aquatic antelope of papyrus and reed swamp, with widely splayed hooves that spread its weight over floating vegetation. Rarely leaves cover, and will submerge to the neck with only its nose and eyes showing when alarmed.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can slow its breathing and stay almost fully submerged in water to hide from predators.
  • Its shaggy, water-shedding coat and elongated, widely spreading hooves are unique among antelope.
  • Best looked for by boat or mokoro through the Okavango's permanent swamps at dawn or dusk.

Springbok

Antidorcas marsupialis

Grazer

A small, sandy-and-white gazelle of the arid Kalahari, superbly adapted to dry country and able to go long periods without drinking. When excited or alarmed it 'pronks' — stiff-legged leaps with the back arched.

See photos
Field notes
  • Pronking can launch it 2 m into the air with a fan of white hair raised on its back.
  • South Africa's national animal, and a Kalahari specialist in Botswana.
  • Once formed migratory herds of millions — among the largest ever recorded.

Steenbok

Raphicerus campestris

Browser

A small, solitary antelope of open grassland and lightly wooded savanna, reddish-fawn with large, mobile ears. Steenbok freeze at the first sign of danger before breaking into a sudden zigzagging dash.

See photos
Field notes
  • Territorial pairs share a home range but usually forage alone, meeting only occasionally.
  • Buries its dung in a shallow scrape, an unusual habit among antelope.
  • Rarely drinks, getting most of its moisture from foliage and roots.

Thomson's Gazelle

Eudorcas thomsonii

Grazer

Small, sprightly gazelle with a bold black side-stripe, tan back, and constantly flickering tail. One of the most abundant and iconic grazers of the Serengeti.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can reach speeds of 80 km/h in short bursts, outpacing most predators

A large, heavily built eagle varying from pale sandy to dark brown, often seen perched on roadside trees or soaring in thermals. Common across Botswana's savanna woodlands.

See photos
Field notes
  • An opportunistic feeder — scavenges, pirates food from other raptors, and hunts small mammals and birds.
  • Nests in large stick platforms in tall trees, reusing the same nest for many years.
  • Frequently confused with the Steppe Eagle, but has a shorter gape line that ends below the middle of the eye.

Wahlberg's Eagle

Hieraaetus wahlbergi

Migratory

A small, slender eagle that arrives as a breeding migrant in spring and summer. Usually dark brown overall with a small crest, though rare pale morphs exist. Often seen soaring above woodland.

See photos
Field notes
  • One of the first summer migrants to arrive, often returning to the same nesting tree each year.
  • Hunts from a soaring position, dropping onto birds, lizards, and small mammals.
  • Nests are small and easily overlooked compared to those of larger eagles.

Western Osprey

Pandion haliaetus

Migratory

A large fish-eating raptor, dark brown above and white below, with a distinctive dark eye-stripe. A non-breeding migrant to Botswana, seen perched near dams, rivers, and floodplains from October to April.

See photos
Field notes
  • Plunge-dives feet-first into water to catch fish, the only raptor that does so regularly.
  • Has a reversible outer toe and spiny foot pads for gripping slippery fish.
  • Migrates from Eurasian breeding grounds, travelling thousands of kilometres each year.

White-backed Vulture

Gyps africanus

Ugly Five

The most common vulture of the region and a vital clean-up crew, descending in squabbling crowds on carcasses. Once abundant, it has crashed to critically endangered status, mainly from poisoning, and every sighting now matters.

See photos
Field notes
  • Soars for hours scanning vast areas, then follows other vultures down to a kill.
  • Numbers have fallen catastrophically across Africa, largely due to poisoning.
  • A whole carcass can be stripped by a descending flock within an hour.

African Darter

Anhinga rufa

Predator

A slender, snake-necked waterbird often seen swimming with just its head and thin neck above the surface, or perched with wings spread wide to dry after diving for fish.

See photos
Field notes
  • Unlike most waterbirds its feathers aren't fully waterproof, so it must spread its wings to dry after every swim.
  • Hunts underwater by stalking fish and spearing them with a quick sideways strike of its sharp bill.
  • Often confused with cormorants, but told apart by its thinner neck, straighter bill and pointed tail.

African Jacana

Actophilornis africanus

A dainty, chestnut waterbird nicknamed the 'lily-trotter', walking on floating vegetation with astonishingly long toes that spread its weight. It picks insects from lily pads across the Okavango's still channels and lagoons.

See photos
Field notes
  • Enormous toes let it stride across floating leaves without sinking.
  • Roles are reversed: the female holds a territory and several males; the males incubate.
  • A father can scoop chicks under his wings and carry them to safety.

African Skimmer

Rynchops flavirostris

Near threatened

A striking tern-like bird with an oversized red-and-black bill, the lower half longer than the upper, used to slice the water's surface for small fish in low, fast flight over open river channels.

See photos
Field notes
  • Feeds by flying low with its longer lower bill cutting the water, snapping shut the instant it touches a fish.
  • Nests in bare scrapes on exposed river sandbanks, making it highly vulnerable to flooding and disturbance.
  • Classified Near Threatened, with the Chobe and Zambezi rivers among its most important strongholds.

African Spoonbill

Platalea alba

Predator

Tall white wading bird with a distinctive flat, spoon-shaped bill and bare red facial skin. Sweeps its bill from side to side through shallow water to filter out small prey.

See photos
Field notes
  • Feeds by sweeping its spatulate bill through water, snapping shut on contact with prey
  • Bare red facial skin and legs intensify in colour during breeding season
  • Nests colonially in trees and reedbeds alongside herons and ibises
  • Bill has sensitive nerve endings that detect prey by touch in murky water

Black Heron

Egretta ardesiaca

Predator

A medium-sized, entirely slate-black heron famous for its unique canopy feeding technique — spreading its wings into an umbrella shape over the water to create shade that attracts fish.

See photos
Field notes
  • The canopy-feeding posture is unmistakable and used by no other heron species.
  • Usually found in shallow floodplains, pans, and the fringes of the Okavango Delta.
  • Often forages alongside other herons and egrets but is much less common.

Black-winged Stilt

Himantopus himantopus

Predator

An elegant black-and-white wader standing on extraordinarily long pink legs, wading belly-deep through the shallows of pans and flooded grassland to pick insects and small aquatic life from the surface.

See photos
Field notes
  • Has the longest legs relative to body size of any bird, letting it feed in water too deep for most waders.
  • Nests in loose colonies on small islands or exposed mud, mobbing any intruder that comes too close.
  • Highly nomadic, moving between wetlands as pans and floodplains fill and dry through the season.

Blacksmith Lapwing

Vanellus armatus

Bold black, white, and grey plover named for its metallic "tink-tink-tink" call that sounds like a blacksmith's hammer on an anvil. Common near water across southern Africa.

See photos
Field notes
  • Named for its ringing "tink-tink" alarm call resembling hammer striking anvil
  • Aggressively dive-bombs predators and even large mammals near its nest
  • Lays eggs directly on open ground, relying on cryptic colouring
  • One of the most commonly seen plovers on safari, found near any open water

Cattle Egret

Bubulcus ibis

A stocky, short-necked white egret almost always seen alongside large mammals — buffalo, elephants, cattle — picking off insects disturbed by their movement. In breeding plumage, develops orange-buff plumes on crown, breast, and back.

See photos
Field notes
  • Often rides on the backs of buffalo and elephants, snatching ticks and flies.
  • The most abundant and widespread egret in the region, found far from water in dry grassland.
  • Roosts communally in large, noisy colonies in waterside trees.

Common Moorhen

Gallinula chloropus

A dark, chicken-like waterbird with a bright red frontal shield and yellow-tipped bill, swimming with a distinctive head-bobbing motion among reeds and lily pads.

See photos
Field notes
  • Swims with vigorous nodding movements, flicking its white-sided tail.
  • Walks across floating vegetation on long, unwebbed toes.
  • Territorial and aggressive — chases rivals with loud, explosive calls.

Cormorant

Phalacrocorax lucidus

Predator

The white-breasted cormorant: a large, mostly black waterbird with a clean white throat and chest, commonly perched upright on dead trees and sandbanks along the Chobe and Okavango waterways, wings often held out to dry.

See photos
Field notes
  • Dives from the surface to chase fish underwater, propelled by strong webbed feet.
  • Often roosts and fishes in loose groups, sometimes alongside darters and herons on the same sandbank.
  • Its plumage wets through like a darter's, so long spells perched with wings spread are needed to dry off.

Egyptian Goose

Alopochen aegyptiaca

The most familiar waterbird of the region, a bold, pale brown goose with a dark eye-patch, rarely far from any river, lagoon or waterhole and often seen loafing on sandbanks in pairs.

See photos
Field notes
  • Pairs mate for life and are fiercely territorial, chasing off rivals and other waterbirds with loud honking.
  • Despite the name it is not a true goose but a large, terrestrial member of the duck family.
  • One of the most widespread waterbirds in Africa, at home on wild floodplains and city park lakes alike.

Goliath Heron

Ardea goliath

Predator

The world's largest heron, standing up to 1.5 metres tall with a rich chestnut head, neck, and breast. A patient solitary hunter found at the edges of lakes, rivers, and marshes.

See photos
Field notes
  • Largest living heron, standing up to 152 cm with a 2.3 m wingspan
  • Always solitary — rarely seen with other goliath herons except when breeding
  • Hunts by standing motionless in shallow water, then striking with its dagger-like bill
  • Can catch and swallow fish weighing over 1 kg

Great White Pelican

Pelecanus onocrotalus

Predator

A massive white waterbird with a huge pinkish bill and throat pouch, often seen in coordinated fishing flocks on the Chobe River and Makgadikgadi Pans.

See photos
Field notes
  • Groups fish cooperatively, forming a semicircle and driving fish into shallow water before scooping them up.
  • Wingspan exceeds 3 metres, making it one of the largest flying birds in Africa.
  • Breeds in large colonies on remote islands and shores, sometimes numbering thousands of pairs.

Greater Flamingo

Phoenicopterus roseus

A tall, pale pink wading bird with a distinctive down-curved bill, filtering tiny crustaceans and algae from shallow alkaline waters. The Makgadikgadi Pans and Sua Pan host large seasonal concentrations.

See photos
Field notes
  • Feeds with its head upside down, using a specialised bill to filter brine shrimp and blue-green algae.
  • Breeds sporadically at Sua Pan when conditions are right — one of only two breeding sites in southern Africa.
  • The pink colour comes entirely from carotenoid pigments in its crustacean diet.

Grey Heron

Ardea cinerea

Predator

A tall, elegant grey-and-white heron with a black eye-stripe and long dagger-like bill, standing motionless at the water's edge before striking at fish with lightning speed.

See photos
Field notes
  • Stands perfectly still for long periods, then strikes with explosive speed to spear fish, frogs, or rodents.
  • Flies with slow, deep wingbeats and its neck retracted into an S-shape.
  • Common along almost every waterway, from floodplains to garden ponds.

Hadada Ibis

Bostrychia hagedash

A heavy-bodied, olive-brown ibis with an iridescent green-purple sheen on the wings and a long, down-curved bill. Unmistakable for its extremely loud, nasal "haa-haa-haa-de-dah" call, often given in flight at dawn and dusk.

See photos
Field notes
  • One of the noisiest birds in Africa — its dawn chorus wakes entire neighbourhoods.
  • Probes soft soil and lawns for earthworms, insects, and spiders with its sensitive bill.
  • Has adapted well to urban areas and is now common in gardens and parks across southern Africa.

Hamerkop

Scopus umbretta

Predator

A modest brown wader with a heavy crest and bill that together form the famous 'hammer head' silhouette. It stalks shallow water for frogs and fish, and builds one of the largest and most elaborate nests of any bird.

See photos
Field notes
  • Builds a giant domed stick nest up to 1.5 m across, strong enough to hold a person.
  • A single nest can contain thousands of sticks and take months to build.
  • Steeped in folklore across Africa as a bird of omen.

Malachite Kingfisher

Corythornis cristatus

Predator

A jewel-bright little kingfisher, no bigger than a sparrow, glowing electric blue above and rich rufous below with a red bill. It perches on reeds over still water and plunges headlong to snatch tiny fish and insects.

See photos
Field notes
  • Among the smallest kingfishers, yet unmistakable for its brilliant colour.
  • Dives from a low perch and returns to beat its catch senseless before swallowing.
  • Nests in a tunnel bored into a vertical riverbank.

Marabou Stork

Leptoptilos crumenifer

Ugly Five

A huge, hunch-shouldered scavenging stork with a bald head, massive bill and a dangling pink throat pouch. Ungainly and often called 'the undertaker', it gathers at kills, fishing camps and towns to clean up carrion and scraps.

See photos
Field notes
  • Wingspan can reach 3.7 m — rivalling the largest of any land bird.
  • The bare head and neck stay clean while feeding inside carcasses.
  • Often soars with vultures, riding thermals to great heights.

Open-billed Stork

Anastomus lamelligerus

Predator

The African openbill: a dark, hunch-backed stork with a distinctive gap between the upper and lower bill even when closed, an adaptation for winkling freshwater snails from their shells.

See photos
Field notes
  • The gap in its bill acts like a pair of tongs, holding a snail shell steady while extracting the flesh.
  • Feeds almost exclusively on aquatic snails, making it a strong indicator of healthy floodplain wetlands.
  • Often gathers in large numbers when a seasonal pan dries down and concentrates its snail prey.

Pied Kingfisher

Ceryle rudis

Predator

A boldly black-and-white kingfisher, unmistakable as it hovers on fast-beating wings over open water before plunging head-first after fish — one of the very few kingfishers that hunts by hovering.

See photos
Field notes
  • One of the few kingfishers able to hover in open air, hanging over the water before diving onto prey.
  • Often hunts in pairs or small family groups, one of the most frequently seen waterbirds on the rivers.
  • Nests in tunnels excavated into sandy riverbanks, sometimes in loose colonies of dozens of pairs.

Reed Cormorant

Microcarbo africanus

Predator

A small, slender, all-dark cormorant with a long tail, commonly seen perching on dead branches over water with wings spread to dry. Much smaller than the White-breasted Cormorant already in this guide.

See photos
Field notes
  • Dives from the surface to catch small fish and frogs in shallow water.
  • Perches with wings outstretched after diving — cormorant feathers are not fully waterproof, allowing deeper dives.
  • Nests in small colonies in trees overhanging water, often with herons and darters.

Saddle-billed Stork

Ephippiorhynchus senegalensis

Predator

The tallest stork in Africa and one of the most beautiful, gleaming black-and-white with an enormous red bill banded in black and topped by a yellow 'saddle'. It stalks shallow floodplains for fish and frogs.

See photos
Field notes
  • The yellow frontal shield on the bill gives it its name.
  • Sexes are told apart by eye colour — yellow in females, dark in males.
  • Uncommon and thinly spread, so a sighting is always a highlight.

Spur-winged Goose

Plectropterus gambensis

Africa's largest waterfowl, a bulky black-and-white goose with a bare red face and a sharp spur on each wing, often grazing in large flocks on floodplain grass well away from water.

See photos
Field notes
  • Carries a sharp bony spur on the bend of each wing, used in fights between rival males.
  • Its flesh can be toxic if it has fed on blister beetles, a local hazard well known to hunters.
  • Forms large flocks on the Okavango and Chobe floodplains, grazing grass much like true geese.

Squacco Heron

Ardeola ralloides

Predator

A small, stocky, buffy-brown heron that looks surprisingly plain at rest but flashes almost pure white wings the instant it takes flight, usually from the reeds at the water's edge.

See photos
Field notes
  • Hunts by standing motionless or slowly stalking at the water's edge, spearing fish, frogs and insects.
  • Its white wings, hidden by folded brown back feathers when perched, are a classic flight giveaway.
  • Often solitary and easily overlooked among reeds until it is flushed into the open.

Whistling Duck

Dendrocygna viduata

The white-faced whistling duck: a gregarious duck with a bold white face and black crown, usually encountered in noisy, tightly packed flocks on lagoons and floodplains, whistling constantly as they fly.

See photos
Field notes
  • Named for its shrill, three-note whistling call, given almost continuously in flight and while feeding.
  • Highly social, roosting and feeding in flocks that can number in the hundreds on a good floodplain.
  • Feeds mostly at night on seeds and aquatic vegetation, often up-ending in shallow water to reach it.

White Stork

Ciconia ciconia

Migratory

A large, elegant black-and-white stork with a bright red bill and legs, arriving as a Palearctic migrant in huge numbers during the wet season. Often seen in loose flocks following ploughs or foraging on burnt grassland.

See photos
Field notes
  • Migrates from Europe each year — one of the most iconic long-distance migrants, covering over 10,000 km.
  • Feeds on insects, frogs, and rodents flushed by grass fires and flooding.
  • Frequently soars in large thermals with other storks and raptors during migration.

Yellow-billed Stork

Mycteria ibis

Predator

A tall white stork with a bright yellow, slightly downcurved bill and a bare red face, often seen sweeping its open bill side to side through shallow water to feel for fish.

See photos
Field notes
  • Hunts by touch, not sight — sweeping its half-open bill through the water and snapping shut on contact.
  • Breeds in large mixed colonies with herons, cormorants and other storks, often in tall waterside trees.
  • Flushes bright pink under the wings in flight, a striking contrast to its otherwise white plumage.

African Hoopoe

Upupa africana

Unmistakable cinnamon-orange bird with a bold black-and-white barred crest that fans open like a crown. Probes the ground with its long, decurved bill for insects and larvae.

See photos
Field notes
  • Erectile crest fans open when alarmed or excited, creating a striking display
  • Probes soil and dung with its long curved bill to extract insect larvae
  • Name is onomatopoeic — call is a soft, carrying "hoo-poo-poo"
  • Nests in tree holes; chicks and incubating females can squirt foul-smelling liquid at intruders

Common Ostrich

Struthio camelus

The largest and fastest-running bird on Earth, a flightless giant of open plains and the Kalahari. It grazes and browses in loose groups, relying on keen eyesight and blistering speed to escape predators.

See photos
Field notes
  • Can sprint at 70 km/h and cover 5 m in a single stride.
  • Lays the largest egg of any living bird — equivalent to about 24 hen's eggs.
  • A powerful forward kick can be lethal, even to a lion.

Fork-tailed Drongo

Dicrurus adsimilis

Glossy black bird with a deeply forked tail and red eye. Fearless and aggressive, it regularly mobbs raptors many times its size and is known for mimicking alarm calls to steal food.

See photos
Field notes
  • Mimics the alarm calls of meerkats and other species to scare them away from food, then steals it
  • Fearlessly attacks eagles, hawks, and even martial eagles in flight
  • Often follows large mammals and ground hornbills to catch flushed insects
  • One of the most intelligent birds in Africa, using over 50 different deceptive calls

Grey Go-away-bird

Corythaixoides concolor

A slim, crested grey bird of thornveld and riverine trees, named for its loud, nasal 'g'way!' call. It is the bush's self-appointed sentinel, and its alarm often gives away hidden predators to hunters and prey alike.

See photos
Field notes
  • Its drawn-out 'go-awaaay' call warns every animal in earshot.
  • Also known as the grey lourie, a relative of the turacos.
  • Clambers acrobatically through outer branches to reach fruit and buds.

Helmeted Guineafowl

Numida meleagris

A plump, ground-dwelling game bird in slate plumage flecked with white spots, topped by a bald blue-and-red head and a bony casque. Noisy flocks scratch through the bush by day and roost in trees at night.

See photos
Field notes
  • Flocks give a loud, cackling alarm that alerts other wildlife to predators.
  • Prefers to run rather than fly, exploding into the air only as a last resort.
  • Widely kept and semi-domesticated as an effective tick and pest controller.

Kori Bustard

Ardeotis kori

One of the heaviest flying birds in the world, a stately grey bustard that strides the open plains and Kalahari in search of insects, reptiles and seeds. It flies only reluctantly, preferring to walk with slow, deliberate steps.

See photos
Field notes
  • Males can weigh up to 19 kg, among the heaviest of all flying birds.
  • In display the male inflates its throat into a great white balloon of feathers.
  • Often seen with carmine bee-eaters riding on its back to catch flushed insects.

Lilac-breasted Roller

Coracias caudatus

A jewel of the bushveld and unofficial favourite of the region, blazing in lilac, turquoise, green and two shades of blue. It perches conspicuously on dead branches, dropping onto insects and small reptiles below.

See photos
Field notes
  • Named for its acrobatic courtship 'rolling' flight display.
  • Reveals dazzling electric-blue wings only in flight.
  • One of the most photographed birds on any southern African safari.

Red-billed Hornbill

Tockus erythrorhynchus

Small hornbill with a slender curved red bill, black-and-white plumage, and a distinctive bobbing walk. One of the most common and recognisable bushveld birds — the inspiration for Zazu in The Lion King.

See photos
Field notes
  • Inspired the character Zazu in Disney's The Lion King
  • Female seals herself inside a tree cavity to nest, fed through a narrow slit by the male
  • Forages on the ground with a characteristic head-bobbing walk
  • Often associates with dwarf mongooses in a mutualistic relationship, sharing sentinel duties

Red-billed Oxpecker

Buphagus erythrorynchus

Small, olive-brown bird with a bright red bill and yellow eye-ring, almost always seen clinging to the hides of large mammals. Feeds on ticks, blood-sucking flies, and wound tissue.

See photos
Field notes
  • Rides on buffalo, giraffe, rhino, and other large mammals, picking off ticks and parasites
  • Relationship is partly parasitic — also feeds on blood from open wounds
  • Hisses loudly when alarmed, alerting its host mammal to approaching predators
  • Nests in tree holes lined with hair plucked from its mammal hosts

Carmine Bee-eater

Merops nubicoides / nubicus

Migratory

A dazzling carmine-and-teal bee-eater that arrives in the dry season to breed in huge colonies, tunnelling nests into river banks along the Zambezi and Okavango. Agile in flight, it hawks bees, wasps and dragonflies on the wing.

See photos
Field notes
  • Thousands nest together in riverbank colonies that riddle the sand with burrows.
  • Rides on the backs of bustards and other large animals to snap up flushed insects.
  • Rubs the sting out of a bee against a branch before swallowing it.
  • Split into a southern form (pink throat) and a northern form (carmine throat); otherwise near-identical.

Southern Ground Hornbill

Bucorvus leadbeateri

Vulnerable

A large, turkey-sized black bird of open savanna, striding in family groups and hunting frogs, snakes and insects with a heavy bill. The vivid red face and throat and booming dawn call are unmistakable.

See photos
Field notes
  • Its deep, resonant booming call is often mistaken for a distant lion.
  • Slow-breeding and long-lived, which makes it vulnerable to habitat loss.
  • Groups cooperate to raise a single chick, with helpers feeding the young.

Southern Yellow-billed Hornbill

Tockus leucomelas

The comical 'flying banana' of the bushveld, a black-and-white hornbill with a heavy curved yellow bill and pale, spotted wings. Tame and inquisitive, it hops around camps and picnic sites hunting insects and scraps.

See photos
Field notes
  • The female seals herself into a tree cavity with mud, fed by the male through a slit.
  • Forages on the ground far more than most other hornbills.
  • Has a mutual-warning partnership with dwarf mongooses while foraging.

Superb Starling

Lamprotornis superbus

Dazzling iridescent starling with a metallic blue-green back, bright orange breast, and a narrow white breast band. One of the most photographed birds in East African safari camps.

See photos
Field notes
  • Iridescent plumage is structural, not pigmented — colour shifts with viewing angle
  • Lives in cooperative groups where non-breeding helpers assist with raising chicks
  • Extremely tame around safari lodges and campsites
  • White breast band separates the blue throat from the orange belly — key field mark

Woodland Kingfisher

Halcyon senegalensis

Migratory

A vivid blue-and-white kingfisher of broadleaved woodland that eats almost no fish at all, instead hunting large insects, lizards and even small birds from an open perch.

See photos
Field notes
  • Its loud, trilling call — a classic sound of the African rains — announces its arrival each year.
  • A summer migrant to much of the region, moving south to breed and heading north again after the rains.
  • Despite the name it hunts almost entirely away from water, dropping onto prey from a bare branch.

African Penguin

Spheniscus demersus

Critically endangered

Africa's only breeding penguin, a braying seabird of the cold Cape coast and offshore islands. Famous from colonies like Boulders Beach, but now sliding fast toward extinction.

See photos
Field notes
  • Once called the jackass penguin for its donkey-like bray.
  • Pink glands above the eyes flush to shed heat in the sun.
  • Uplisted to Critically Endangered in 2024 as numbers collapse.

Grey Crowned Crane

Balearica regulorum

Endangered

A spectacular wetland crane crowned with a halo of stiff golden feathers, with a red throat wattle and pale grey body. The national bird of Uganda, dancing on open marshland.

See photos
Field notes
  • Uganda's national bird, shown on its flag and coat of arms.
  • The only crane that roosts in trees, thanks to a grasping hind toe.
  • Pairs dance — bowing, jumping and tossing grass — year-round.

Shoebill

Balaeniceps rex

Vulnerable

A prehistoric-looking giant of papyrus swamps, slate-grey with a huge clog-shaped bill. It stands motionless for hours before lunging at lungfish — a bucket-list bird of Uganda's wetlands.

See photos
Field notes
  • The massive bill is hooked and sharp-edged for seizing lungfish.
  • Hunts by standing frozen, then striking in an explosive lunge.
  • Best sought in the Mabamba and Bangweulu swamps.

Somali Ostrich

Struthio molybdophanes

Vulnerable

The blue-necked ostrich of the Horn and northern Kenya, split from the common ostrich in 2014. Breeding males show grey-blue, not pink, bare skin on the neck and legs.

See photos
Field notes
  • Breeding males have blue-grey, not pink, neck and thighs.
  • Recognised as a full species, separate from the common ostrich, in 2014.
  • Replaces the common ostrich north and east of the Tana River.

Abyssinian Ground Hornbill

Bucorvus abyssinicus

Vulnerable

A turkey-sized ground hornbill of the Sahel and East African savannas, black with a tall casque and a bare, inflatable throat. It stalks the grass hunting prey on foot.

See photos
Field notes
  • Walks tens of kilometres a day hunting insects, reptiles and small mammals.
  • The male's throat skin is blue and red; the female's all blue.
  • Reaches its south-eastern limit in Kenya and north-east Uganda.

Baobab

Adansonia digitata

Africa's most iconic tree — a massive, bottle-shaped trunk that can reach 10 m or more across, topped by a crown of bare, root-like branches for most of the year. Known across the region as the "tree of life" or "upside-down tree".

See photos
Field notes
  • Can live well over a thousand years and store thousands of litres of water in its spongy trunk.
  • Flowers open at dusk and are pollinated mainly by fruit bats; each flower lasts only a single night.
  • The fruit's tangy pulp is rich in vitamin C and widely eaten; elephants strip the fibrous bark for moisture in the dry season.

Camelthorn

Vachellia erioloba

A tall, flat-crowned acacia of the Kalahari sandveld, casting some of the only shade for kilometres across open pans and dune country. Long, straight white thorns and dark, twisted seed pods make it easy to identify.

See photos
Field notes
  • Its taproot can reach over 60 m deep in search of water, among the deepest of any tree.
  • Sociable weavers build huge communal nests weighing hundreds of kilograms in its branches.
  • Fallen seed pods are a favourite dry-season food for gemsbok, eland and kudu.

Fever Tree

Vachellia xanthophloea

Striking acacia with a smooth, powdery yellow-green bark that almost glows in sunlight. Early settlers linked it to malaria — hence "fever tree" — because it grows near swampy areas where mosquitoes breed.

See photos
Field notes
  • Yellow-green bark contains photosynthetic tissue, producing energy even when leafless
  • Named "fever tree" because settlers associated its swampy habitat with malaria
  • Thorns are long, straight, and white, arranged in pairs at each node
  • Grows rapidly, reaching up to 25 metres, often forming beautiful gallery forests along waterways

Jackalberry

Diospyros mespiliformis

A tall, evergreen riverine giant found along the Okavango, Chobe and Zambezi, with dark, fissured bark and glossy leaves that give deep, welcome shade — often the shade tree of choice for safari camps.

See photos
Field notes
  • Its sweet, date-like fruit is relished by baboons, warthog, kudu and elephant, which help disperse the seeds.
  • Often grows alongside sausage trees and fig trees as a marker of underground water or a watercourse.
  • The heavy, dark timber has long been prized locally for furniture and carving.

Leadwood

Combretum imberbe

A slow-growing giant of Chobe and Okavango woodland with pale, flaking, deeply fissured bark and exceptionally dense, heavy timber that barely floats — hence the name.

See photos
Field notes
  • Among the longest-lived trees in the region, with mature specimens estimated at over a thousand years old.
  • Even after death its hard timber can stand for centuries, whitened and bone-like, a familiar landmark on floodplains.
  • Traditionally valued for tools, ash used as a cosmetic, and as a source of very slow-burning firewood.

Marula

Sclerocarya birrea

Iconic African fruit tree with a spreading grey-barked crown. Its sweet yellow fruit is prized by elephants, eaten by dozens of species, and famously used to make Amarula cream liqueur.

See photos
Field notes
  • Fruit is the base for Amarula cream liqueur, a well-known South African export
  • Elephants are major seed dispersers, eating fallen fruit in large quantities
  • Each tree is either male or female — only females bear fruit
  • Bark, leaves, and fruit have traditional medicinal uses across Africa

Mopane

Colophospermum mopane

The dominant tree of the hot, dry lowveld across much of Zimbabwe and northern Botswana, easily known by its distinctive butterfly-shaped leaflets that fold together in the midday heat to cut water loss.

See photos
Field notes
  • Forms vast, near single-species 'mopane woodlands', a key habitat for elephant and browsing antelope.
  • Its leaves are the sole food of the mopane worm, a large edible caterpillar and important local food source.
  • Elephants can browse mopane so heavily it coppices into dense, stunted 'mopane scrub'.

Sausage Tree

Kigelia africana

Unmistakable tree with enormous, sausage-shaped fruits up to 60 cm long hanging on rope-like stalks. Deep red flowers open at night and are pollinated by bats.

See photos
Field notes
  • Fruits can weigh up to 10 kg and are toxic when raw — never park under a sausage tree
  • Flowers open at night and are pollinated by fruit bats attracted to their musty scent
  • Widely used in traditional medicine for skin conditions and as a cosmetic
  • Hippos, baboons, and elephants eat the fallen fruit
